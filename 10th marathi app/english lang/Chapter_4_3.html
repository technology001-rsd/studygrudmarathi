subjective
✅ Answer:
(a) Signs of celebration: The bells are ringing, people are exulting, crowds are swaying with eager faces, bouquets and ribbon'd wreaths are ready, the flag is flung, and the bugle trills. (b) The crowds are "a-crowding" on the shores, they are calling for the Captain, their faces are eager, and they have prepared bouquets and wreaths to honour him. (c) Signs that the Captain has passed away: "His lips are pale and still"; "He has no pulse nor will"; "He does not feel my arm"; "Fallen cold and dead." (d) The speaker puts his arm under the Captain's head as a gesture of love, care and respect, hoping to comfort him even in death — as a son would care for his father. (e) The speaker is deeply grief-stricken. He addresses the Captain with desperate hope: "rise up and hear the bells." He cannot accept the reality and calls it "some dream." Even after the victory is won, his heart bleeds. He walks the deck with "mournful tread," consumed by sorrow while the world celebrates.
objective
✅ Answer:
In the first two stanzas, the speaker addresses the dead Captain (Abraham Lincoln) directly — "O Captain! My Captain!" — pleading with him to rise up and see the celebrations. In the last stanza, the speaker addresses the shores and bells — "Exult, O shores, and ring, O bells!" — telling the world to celebrate, while he walks sadly on the deck. He also speaks about the Captain in third person: "My Captain does not answer."

✅ Answer:
Title: O Captain! My Captain! Poet: Walt Whitman The poem is an elegy written in memory of President Abraham Lincoln after his assassination. It uses the extended metaphor of a ship returning safely from a voyage to represent the USA after winning the Civil War. The Captain represents Lincoln, who led the nation through the war but was killed just as victory was achieved. Rhyme scheme: aabbcded. The poem has three stanzas, each ending with the refrain "Fallen cold and dead." The central theme is the contrast between public celebration and personal grief. While the nation rejoices in victory, the speaker mourns the loss of its great leader. Figures of speech used include Apostrophe ("O Captain! My Captain!"), Personification (Triumph and Disaster as impostors), Metaphor (ship = nation, Captain = Lincoln), Alliteration ("flag is flung"), Repetition ("fallen cold and dead"), and Antithesis (celebration vs mourning). The language is emotional, rhythmic and powerful. The poem moves from desperate hope ("rise up") to sad acceptance ("does not answer"). I like this poem because it beautifully captures the mixed feelings of victory and loss, and honours a great leader in a touching way.
